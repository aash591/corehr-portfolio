Working hours, leave & holidays: what Kerala law requires

How long employees can legally work, what overtime must pay, how much leave they're entitled to, and how many paid holidays Kerala law sets — plus where common practice goes further than the law.

🏛️ Kerala S&E Act, Factories Act 1948, Kerala NFH Act 1958 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The legal limits on daily and weekly working hours, the mandatory overtime rate, statutory leave entitlements, and the number of paid national/festival holidays Kerala law requires.
Why does it matter?
These are the most commonly checked, most visible compliance items in any inspection — and the most likely to differ from what a business has been informally doing for years.
Who does it apply to?
Every employer. Shops, offices and commercial establishments follow the Kerala Shops and Commercial Establishments Act; factories follow the Factories Act, 1948. Both are separate from Kerala's National and Festival Holidays Act, which applies broadly across both.
What should an employer do first?
Confirm which Act applies to your establishment (shop/office vs. factory), then check your current working hours, leave policy and holiday calendar against that Act's minimums.

Applicability: which law covers you

Most Core HR clients — shops, offices, IT and service businesses, retail — are covered by the Kerala Shops and Commercial Establishments Act, 1960. This applies from your very first employee; there's no headcount threshold for the working-hours and leave provisions.

A manufacturing unit or workshop that meets the Factories Act's definition (broadly, 10 or more workers with power-driven machinery, or 20 or more without) is covered by the Factories Act, 1948 instead, which has its own, slightly different limits.

Separately, the Kerala Industrial Establishments (National and Festival Holidays) Act, 1958 sets a minimum number of paid holidays and applies broadly across both categories of establishment.

"Ordinary rate of wages" for overtime calculation means basic pay plus regular allowances the employee is entitled to — it excludes bonus and any overtime already earned. Both Acts are firm on the 2x rate; there's no statutory route to pay overtime at a lower multiple.

Weekly holiday

Both Acts require at least one full day of rest in every week — an employee cannot be worked seven days straight, even with overtime consent. This weekly holiday is separate from, and in addition to, the paid leave and festival holidays covered below.

Under the Kerala Shops and Commercial Establishments Act, sick leave and casual leave are two separate 12-day pools — not one combined bucket — and both typically lapse at the end of the calendar year if unused, unlike earned leave which can carry forward.

Practice vs. law: the figures above are legal minimums. Many Kerala employers, especially in IT, professional services and larger companies, offer more than this — common practice is 15–21 days of combined annual leave, sometimes with separate sick leave on top, and unlimited or looser casual leave policies. None of that extra is legally required; it's a retention and hiring decision, not a compliance one. What the law does require is that you never fall below the statutory minimum.

National and Festival Holidays

The Kerala Industrial Establishments (National and Festival Holidays) Act, 1958 sets a statutory floor of 13 paid holidays a year: 4 fixed national holidays — 26 January (Republic Day), 1 May (May Day), 15 August (Independence Day), and 2 October (Gandhi Jayanti) — plus 9 further festival holidays, decided locally in consultation between the employer, employees, and the labour inspector.

This is a floor, not a ceiling. Many Kerala employers, particularly larger companies and IT/service businesses, publish a holiday calendar well above 13 — commonly in the high teens to low twenties once regional festivals, optional holidays, and company-specific days are added. That's common practice, not a separate legal requirement beyond the 13-day statutory minimum.

An employee required to work on a national or festival holiday is generally entitled to a substitute holiday or additional compensation, depending on how your establishment has structured its holiday calendar — this should be set out clearly in your holiday policy rather than decided case by case.

Employer responsibilities

  • Fix and display working hours, weekly holiday, and rest intervals as required by the applicable Act.
  • Pay overtime at double the ordinary rate for every hour beyond the daily/weekly limit — never absorb it into a flat monthly salary without a clear, compliant basis for doing so.
  • Track earned leave, sick leave and casual leave separately, and don't let policy documents silently fall below the statutory minimum for any one of them.
  • Publish and follow a National and Festival Holidays calendar of at least 13 days, agreed in the manner the Act contemplates.
  • Maintain attendance and overtime registers — this is what's actually checked at inspection, more often than the policy document itself.

Worked example: overtime pay

Worked example

An employee at a Kerala shop earns ₹18,000/month in basic + allowances that count toward "ordinary rate of wages," working a standard 26-day month.

  • Ordinary daily rate: ₹18,000 ÷ 26 ≈ ₹692
  • Ordinary hourly rate (8-hour day): ₹692 ÷ 8 ≈ ₹86.50
  • Overtime rate (double): ₹86.50 × 2 ≈ ₹173/hour
  • If the employee works 6 hours of overtime in a week: 6 × ₹173 ≈ ₹1,038 owed on top of regular wages

This is a simplified illustration — the exact "ordinary rate of wages" calculation depends on which allowances your pay structure counts as wages for this purpose.

Non-compliance and correction

Under the Kerala Shops and Commercial Establishments Act, contravening the working-hours, leave or holiday provisions can draw a fine of up to ₹50,000 for the core violations (working hours, leave, holidays among them), with a lower fine band for registration-related lapses. Under the Factories Act, a general violation can draw imprisonment of up to 2 years, a fine of up to ₹1,00,000, or both — with a further daily fine for a continuing violation, and materially higher fines where a breach causes death or serious injury.

Found a gap? Correct the working pattern and pay any overtime shortfall going forward, and settle any leave/holiday deficit for the current cycle. Most working-hours and leave gaps are easy to fix once identified — the risk is in letting an informal, non-compliant pattern continue for years before it's checked.

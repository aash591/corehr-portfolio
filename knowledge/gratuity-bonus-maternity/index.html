Gratuity, bonus & maternity benefits: the complete guide

Three separate statutory entitlements, three different laws, three different thresholds. Here's exactly what Kerala employers owe employees under each — and what it costs to get it wrong.

🏛️ Central laws — Payment of Gratuity Act 1972, Payment of Bonus Act 1965, Maternity Benefit Act 1961 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 14 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
Three unrelated statutory benefits: gratuity (a lump sum for long service), statutory bonus (a minimum annual payout linked to wages, not just profit), and maternity benefit (paid leave and protections around childbirth).
Why does it matter?
Each has its own eligibility test, its own formula, and its own penalty regime. Being compliant on one doesn't tell you anything about the other two — they need to be checked separately.
Who does it apply to?
Gratuity and maternity benefit: establishments with 10 or more employees. Statutory bonus: establishments with 20 or more employees. Thresholds are independent.
What should an employer do first?
Check your headcount against each of the three thresholds separately, and confirm which current employees actually qualify under each scheme.
Recent development

Parliament has folded the Payment of Gratuity Act, the Payment of Bonus Act, and the Maternity Benefit Act into the Code on Social Security, 2020 and the Code on Wages, 2019. These Codes have had legal effect centrally since 21 November 2025, and the Union Government notified its central rules in May 2026.

Kerala has not yet notified the state-level rules needed to bring the Codes into practical effect here — the state government has publicly said it needs more time to study them. Until Kerala notifies its rules, the original Acts named above remain what Kerala employers follow in practice, and the eligibility rules, formulas and ceilings in this article reflect those original Acts. One change worth knowing about regardless: the Code on Social Security lets fixed-term employees claim gratuity without completing 5 years once their contract ends — this isn't yet operative in Kerala, but it's worth watching if you use fixed-term contracts.

Gratuity: eligibility and formula

Applicability: the Payment of Gratuity Act applies to every factory, mine, plantation, port and railway company, and to any shop or establishment employing 10 or more people on any day in the preceding 12 months. Once an establishment is covered, it stays covered even if headcount later falls below 10.

Eligibility: an employee qualifies after 5 years of continuous service. The 5-year rule is waived only if employment ends because of death or disablement — in that case, gratuity is paid to the employee or their nominee regardless of how long they worked.

Formula: (last drawn basic pay + dearness allowance) × 15 × completed years of service ÷ 26. A period of service of 6 months or more beyond a completed year is rounded up to the next full year.

Ceiling: the maximum gratuity payable, and the amount that's tax-free in the employee's hands, is currently ₹20 lakh. This was raised from ₹10 lakh in 2018. An employer can choose to pay more than the ceiling, but anything beyond ₹20 lakh is added to the employee's taxable income for that year and isn't compelled by the Act.

Payment timeline: gratuity must be paid within 30 days of it becoming due. If it isn't, the employer owes simple interest on the delayed amount, at a rate notified by the government (10% per annum, under the applicable notification), for the entire delay period.

Worked example — gratuity

An employee's last drawn basic pay + DA is ₹30,000/month, with 12 completed years of service.

Gratuity = ₹30,000 × 15 × 12 ÷ 26 = ₹2,07,692

This is well under the ₹20 lakh ceiling, so the full amount is payable and tax-free in the employee's hands.

Statutory bonus: eligibility and calculation

Applicability: the Payment of Bonus Act applies to every factory, and every other establishment employing 20 or more people on any day during the accounting year. Once covered, an establishment generally stays covered even if headcount later falls.

Eligibility: an employee whose wages (basic + DA) are ₹21,000/month or less qualifies, provided they've worked at least 30 days in that accounting year.

Calculation ceiling: even for an eligible employee, bonus is calculated on wages capped at ₹7,000/month, or the minimum wage notified for that job if it's higher than ₹7,000 — whichever of the two is greater becomes the calculation base.

Rate: the statutory minimum is 8.33% of the calculation-base wage for the year, and it's payable even in a loss-making year — there's a narrow exemption for genuinely new establishments in their first few years. The maximum is 20%, and where it should land between 8.33% and 20% depends on the employer's "allocable surplus" as computed under the Act's formula.

Payment window: statutory bonus must be paid within 8 months of the close of the accounting year. For a business on the April–March financial year, that's by 30 November.

Worked example — bonus

An employee's wages (basic + DA) are ₹18,000/month. They're eligible (under ₹21,000), but the bonus is calculated on the ₹7,000 ceiling, not the actual ₹18,000.

  • Annual calculation base: ₹7,000 × 12 = ₹84,000
  • Minimum bonus (8.33%): ₹84,000 × 8.33% ≈ ₹7,000 for the year
  • Maximum bonus (20%), if the employer's surplus supports it: ₹84,000 × 20% = ₹16,800 for the year

Maternity benefit: leave and protections

Applicability: the Maternity Benefit Act applies to factories, mines, plantations, and any shop or establishment employing 10 or more people. It covers women employees who've worked at least 80 days in the 12 months immediately before their expected delivery date.

Leave entitlement: 26 weeks of paid leave for the first two children — up to 8 weeks can be taken before the expected delivery date, with the rest taken after. For a third child onward, the entitlement drops to 12 weeks (6 before, 6 after). Leave for miscarriage or medical termination is 6 weeks; for a tubectomy operation, 2 weeks.

Adoption and surrogacy: a mother adopting a child below 3 months of age, or a commissioning mother in a surrogacy arrangement, is entitled to 12 weeks of paid leave from the date the child is handed over.

Crèche facility: an establishment with 50 or more employees must provide a crèche, and a mother returning to work is entitled to 4 visits to the crèche a day, including rest intervals.

Work from home: after the paid leave period ends, the employer may allow the employee to work from home for a further period, depending on the nature of her work and by mutual agreement. It's an enabling option in the Act, not an automatic entitlement.

Medical bonus: if the employer doesn't provide free pre-natal and post-natal medical care, the employee is entitled to a medical bonus, currently ₹3,500 as last notified.

Protection: an employer cannot dismiss an employee, or vary her employment to her disadvantage, because she is on or has applied for maternity leave.

Employer responsibilities

Across all three schemes, the running obligations are similar in spirit even though the mechanics differ:

  • Gratuity: maintain service records accurate enough to calculate years of service correctly, pay within 30 days of it becoming due, and consider a group gratuity insurance policy to fund the liability instead of carrying it as an unfunded balance sheet risk.
  • Bonus: compute and pay the minimum bonus within 8 months of year-end even without checking profitability first, and maintain the registers the Act requires (showing computation of allocable surplus, set-on/set-off, and bonus paid to each employee).
  • Maternity benefit: confirm the employee's 80-day eligibility, pay maternity benefit in advance for the pre-delivery portion of leave and the balance promptly after, keep the role open on return, and provide the crèche facility if headcount requires it.

Non-compliance and correction

  • Gratuity: delayed payment attracts simple interest for the full delay period. Non-payment can also lead to prosecution under the Act, in addition to a direction to pay the amount due.
  • Bonus: failure to pay the minimum bonus is an offence under the Act, and unpaid bonus can be recovered as arrears of wages through the labour authority.
  • Maternity benefit: denying or discharging an employee in contravention of the Act can lead to imprisonment and/or fine for the employer, in addition to the maternity benefit itself remaining due.

Found a gap? Pay what's owed with the applicable interest before it's raised as a claim. These are three of the most employee-visible obligations an employer has — an unpaid or delayed gratuity, bonus, or maternity benefit is also one of the fastest routes to a labour department complaint.

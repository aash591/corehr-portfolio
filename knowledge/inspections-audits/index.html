Inspections & Compliance Audits: the complete employer guide

Who can walk into your establishment and check your records, what they look for, how the Shram Suvidha Portal has changed the process, and why running your own audit first beats reacting to theirs.

🏛️ EPF, ESI, Shops Act & Factories Act inspections 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
A statutory inspection is a check by a government-appointed officer — under the PF Act, ESI Act, Kerala Shops Act, or Factories Act — to verify that an establishment is registered, paying its dues, and maintaining required records.
Why does it matter?
Inspectors have wide legal powers to enter premises, examine records, and question staff. A poorly prepared inspection can turn a minor paperwork gap into a show-cause notice, penalty, or worse.
Who does it apply to?
Every registered establishment — the specific inspecting authority depends on which laws apply to you (PF, ESI, Shops Act, Factories Act, or all of them).
What should an employer do first?
Run an internal compliance audit against the same checklist an inspector would use, before one shows up — this is the single most effective way to avoid inspection-day surprises.

Who can inspect your business

In Kerala, four kinds of inspecting authority can visit a covered establishment, each under a different law.

AuthorityGoverning ActWhat they check
EPFO Enforcement OfficerEmployees' Provident Funds & Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952PF registration, contribution payments, coverage of eligible employees, wage records
ESIC InspectorEmployees' State Insurance Act, 1948ESI registration, contribution payments, employee coverage, accident/injury records
Kerala Labour Department InspectorKerala Shops & Commercial Establishments Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Wages Act, Kerala Labour Welfare Fund ActRegistration certificate, working hours, leave records, wage payment, LWF contributions
Factories & Boilers InspectorFactories Act, 1948; Indian Boilers Act, 1923; Kerala Factories Rules, 1957Safety records, working conditions, boiler certification, statutory registers

A single business can be visited by more than one of these, since each authority acts independently under its own Act. A shop with a factory licence and 25 employees, for example, can in principle be checked by all four.

The Shram Suvidha Portal and how inspections are chosen

The Ministry of Labour & Employment's Unified Shram Suvidha Portal covers the Office of the Chief Labour Commissioner, EPFO, ESIC, and the Directorate General of Mines Safety. It doesn't stop inspections, but it does change how they're chosen.

Under its Transparent Labour Inspection Scheme, physical inspections are selected through a computerised, risk-based, randomised process, rather than an inspector's own discretion. Once an inspection happens, the officer must upload the inspection report on the portal within a short window — 48 to 72 hours depending on the scheme — so the outcome is visible and traceable rather than left to informal follow-up.

Complaint-driven inspections sit outside this randomisation — if a specific complaint or violation is reported, the authority can still inspect directly, whatever the risk score says.

Self-certification: who's exempt, and who isn't

Self-certification schemes let an eligible establishment file its own compliance declaration instead of facing routine inspection. In Kerala, this exists in two narrow forms, not as a general exemption.

  • SEZ units: the Kerala Government has permitted units in its Special Economic Zones (such as CSEZ) to self-certify compliance under specified labour laws, with the Labour Commissioner and Director of Factories & Boilers discontinuing routine inspection for units that file self-certificates. Complaint-driven inspection still applies if a specific violation is reported.
  • New MSMEs: a central government advisory allows newly set-up micro, small and medium enterprises to self-certify compliance under 6 specified labour laws for their first 3 years, filing a single combined return instead of separate ones.

Outside these two categories, a standard Kerala establishment — a running shop, office, restaurant, or small factory past its first 3 years — is not automatically exempt from inspection. Registration, contribution payment, and record-keeping still need to hold up to a visit at any time.

What inspectors typically look for

  • Valid registration certificates — PF code, ESI code, Shops Act certificate (and renewal status), factory licence if applicable.
  • Statutory registers — attendance, wages, leave, overtime, and the employment register required under the applicable Act.
  • Proof of timely contribution payment — PF and ESI challans, Professional Tax and Labour Welfare Fund remittance records.
  • Wage compliance — actual wages paid against the current Kerala minimum wage notification for that category of work.
  • Working conditions — display of statutory notices/abstracts, working hours records, leave availed vs. entitlement.
  • For factories — safety records, machine guarding, boiler certification, and accident register.
  • Exit records — F&F settlement proof and PF/ESI exit marking for employees who have left, since gaps here are a common red flag.

Preparing for and responding to an inspection

Most inspections are less stressful when the paperwork is simply in order. A few practical steps make the difference:

  • Keep registers current, not reconstructed later. An inspector can usually tell the difference, and a register filled in after the fact is worse than an honest gap.
  • Designate one person — typically HR or the proprietor — as the point of contact so the inspector isn't redirected between people.
  • Cooperate and provide what's asked — obstruction or refusal to produce records is itself an offence under most of these Acts, separate from any underlying compliance gap.
  • Get a copy of the inspection report or note before the officer leaves, if one is issued on the spot.
  • Respond to any show-cause notice within the given time — silence is read as an admission far more often than a timely, factual reply.

What happens after an inspection

StageWhat it means
Inspection reportThe officer's written record of what was checked and any gaps found — uploaded to the Shram Suvidha Portal within the prescribed window.
Show-cause noticeIssued if a violation is recorded; gives the employer a fixed period to explain or correct the gap.
Compliance/recovery orderIf the explanation isn't accepted, or the gap isn't corrected, the authority can order payment of the shortfall plus interest/damages.
Penalty or prosecutionFor serious or repeated non-compliance, most of these Acts allow monetary penalty and, in some cases, prosecution of the employer.

Building a recurring internal audit habit

New branch or location openedFresh registrations/updates at the new local body or jurisdiction

After any exitF&F paid on time, PF/ESI exit correctly marked

A proactive audit finds gaps on your own schedule, with time to fix them quietly. Waiting for a government inspection means the same gaps surface with a show-cause notice, a penalty clock, and an official record attached from day one — this is the core reason a periodic internal audit is worth budgeting for even when nothing seems wrong.

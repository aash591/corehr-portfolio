Safety & Records

Employment records, registers & returns

The paperwork that backs up every other compliance area. If your registers are clean, everything else — payroll, PF, ESI, inspections — gets easier.

🏛️ Multiple Acts — central and Kerala 📅 Last verified: 20 September 2026 ⏱️ 7 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The set of documents and registers every employer should keep: appointment letters, attendance and wage records, statutory registers, workplace notices, and returns to various authorities.
Why does it matter?
These records are what an inspector checks first, and what you rely on if a wage, leave, or termination dispute ever comes up.
Who does it apply to?
Every employer. The exact registers required depend on which Acts apply to you — Shops Act, Factories Act, PF, ESI and others each add their own.
What should an employer do first?
Check you have the core registers (attendance, wages, leave) in place and up to date, and that appointment letters exist for every current employee.

Applicability

Record-keeping requirements come from several Acts at once, not one single law. Which registers you need depends on what applies to your business: a Kerala Shops & Commercial Establishments registration brings its own register requirements, a factory needs a muster roll under the Factories Act, and PF/ESI registration bring their own return and record obligations.

In practice, almost every employer needs the same core set: attendance, wages, and leave records, at minimum.

Employee rights

Good records protect employees too. A wage register that clearly shows what was paid and why is what an employee relies on if they dispute a deduction or a leave balance. An appointment letter is their main written proof of their employment terms.

Employer responsibilities and benefits

Issue appointment letters, maintain the required registers accurately and on an ongoing basis, display the notices your applicable Acts require, and retain records for the required period. Clean records are also what makes due diligence, funding, and audits go smoothly — messy records slow all three down.

Appointment letters

There's no single national form every employer must use, but a written appointment letter is standard practice and, in effect, a practical necessity. It should cover:

  • Role, reporting structure, and work location
  • Salary structure and payment cycle
  • Working hours and leave entitlement
  • Notice period for resignation and termination
  • Probation terms, if applicable

Without one, you have far less to rely on if a dispute arises over what was actually agreed.

PF, ESI, Professional Tax and Labour Welfare Fund each bring their own additional records — contribution challans, employee enrolment details, and filing acknowledgements. See the individual guides for those.

Notices to display

Most applicable Acts require a notice or abstract to be displayed at the workplace: a summary of the Act itself, the working hours in force, the weekly holiday, and the holiday list for the year. This is a small, easy-to-miss requirement that inspectors do check.

Record retention

How long to keep records depends on the Act, but a safe working rule is to keep payroll and statutory records for at least the retention period of the strictest applicable Act — commonly several years, and longer for PF- and gratuity-related records, since employees can raise claims well after leaving. We recommend keeping core payroll and register records for a minimum of 8 years, and PF/ESI-linked records for longer.

Non-compliance and correction

Missing or incomplete registers are one of the most common findings in a labour inspection. On their own they usually lead to a notice to correct and produce records — but gaps often prompt closer scrutiny of payroll, contributions, and working hours too, which is where the real risk lies.

Found gaps? Reconstruct what you can from payroll and bank records, and put a proper register system in place going forward. This is one of the easiest compliance areas to fix once you decide to fix it.

Salary TDS: what employers must deduct and file

Every employer paying salary above the tax-free threshold must estimate each employee's annual tax, deduct it monthly, deposit it, and report it every quarter. Here's how the mechanics actually work.

🏛️ Central law — Income-tax Act, 2025 (Section 392; formerly Section 192) 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The employer's duty to estimate each employee's total tax for the year, deduct it from salary every month, deposit it with the government, and report it in a quarterly statement.
Why does it matter?
It isn't optional withholding — if you deduct too little or deposit late, the employer (not the employee) is the one who pays interest and can be treated as being "in default."
Who does it apply to?
Every employer, for every employee whose estimated annual salary income exceeds the exemption limit. There's no minimum headcount.
What should an employer do first?
Collect each employee's tax regime declaration and investment proofs at the start of the year, and re-estimate the annual tax whenever pay changes.
Recent change

The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026, replacing the 1961 Act. Salary TDS moved from Section 192 to Section 392, and the quarterly statement moved from Form 24Q to Form 138. The renumbering didn't change any rate, threshold, or due date — it changed the labels you must now use on challans and returns.

If you're filing for a period before 1 April 2026, the old section and form numbers still apply. For FY 2026-27 onward, use Section 392 and Form 138.

Applicability

This applies to every employer — company, partnership, proprietorship, or individual — who pays salary to an employee. There's no headcount threshold, unlike PF or ESI.

The obligation is triggered per employee, not per business. You deduct tax only for an employee whose estimated income for the year, after allowable exemptions and deductions under their chosen regime, exceeds the basic exemption limit. An employee below that estimated threshold has no TDS deducted at all.

Multiple employers in one year: if an employee joins you mid-year after working elsewhere, they can disclose their previous salary and TDS to you in writing, and you factor that into your estimate so the correct total tax is deducted across both employers.

Employee rights

An employee has the right to choose their tax regime for the year and declare it to you in writing. They're entitled to see the deduction clearly on their payslip, and to receive Form 16 — the annual TDS certificate — by 15 June each year.

They can check what's actually been deposited in their name through Form 26AS or the Annual Information Statement (AIS) on the income tax portal. If more tax was deducted than actually due, they claim the difference back as a refund when filing their return.

Employer responsibilities

Core obligations, in order:

  • Obtain a Tax Deduction and Collection Account Number (TAN) — this is separate from your business PAN.
  • Collect each employee's regime declaration and, if they choose the old regime, their investment/exemption proofs.
  • Estimate each employee's total salary income for the year and compute the tax payable under their chosen (or default) regime.
  • Deduct roughly one-twelfth of that estimated annual tax from salary each month, adjusting later months as actual pay, bonus, or investment proof changes the estimate.
  • Deposit the deducted tax with the government by the due date.
  • File the quarterly TDS statement (Form 138, formerly Form 24Q).
  • Issue Form 16 to every employee from whom tax was deducted, by 15 June.

Getting this right also protects the business: a wrong or missed deduction becomes the employer's liability first, with interest running from the date it should have happened — recoverable from the employee later, but the employer pays it up front and chases the recovery separately.

Old regime vs new regime

An employee can choose between two tax regimes each year: the old regime, which allows exemptions and deductions (HRA, 80C investments, home loan interest, and others) against proof submitted to you, and the new regime, which has fewer deductions but different slab structure.

Since FY 2023-24, the new regime is the default. If an employee doesn't submit a written declaration choosing the old regime, you deduct TDS under the new regime automatically. A salaried employee can switch their choice each financial year — it isn't locked in permanently the way it is for some business income.

Practically: send out a regime-declaration form at the start of each financial year (or on joining, for a new hire), and don't accept verbal instructions — keep the written declaration on file, since it's what you'd need to show if the deduction is ever questioned.

How the deduction is actually calculated

There's no flat percentage for salary TDS. Instead, the employer works out, at the start of the year (and re-checks through the year), roughly this:

  1. Estimate the employee's total salary for the financial year — basic, allowances, perquisites, bonus if reasonably known.
  2. Subtract standard deduction and any exemptions/deductions allowed under the regime the employee has chosen (old regime needs proof; new regime allows very few).
  3. Apply the income tax slab rates currently notified for that financial year to arrive at estimated annual tax.
  4. Add applicable cess.
  5. Divide that annual tax across the remaining pay periods in the year, and deduct that amount each month.

Slab rates change almost every year in the Union Budget — sometimes for both regimes, sometimes just one. This is normal, not something to work around: check the current slab rates published on the Income Tax Department's e-filing portal (incometax.gov.in) at the start of each financial year, and again if a mid-year Budget change affects the current year. Don't rely on a rate you used last year without confirming it's still current.

Worked example (illustrative)

An employee's projected annual salary is ₹9,00,000. After standard deduction and any exemptions allowed under their chosen regime, suppose their computed annual tax liability works out to ₹40,000 under that year's applicable slab rates.

  • Monthly TDS (spread evenly): ₹40,000 ÷ 12 ≈ ₹3,333
  • If the employee receives a bonus in month 8 that raises their annual estimate, the employer recomputes total tax due for the year and adjusts the remaining months' deduction upward to catch up — rather than waiting until March.

The ₹40,000 figure here is only to illustrate the mechanism. Actual tax depends on the slab rates and regime in force for that specific financial year — always compute from the current published rates, not a fixed number carried over from a previous year.

Non-compliance and correction

  • Late deduction: interest at 1% per month (or part of a month) from the date tax should have been deducted to the date it actually was, under Section 398 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (formerly Section 201(1A)).
  • Late deposit: interest at 1.5% per month (or part of a month) from the date of deduction to the date of actual deposit, under the same section.
  • Late filing of the quarterly statement: a fee of ₹200 per day of delay, capped at the TDS amount for that quarter.
  • Incorrect or seriously delayed statements: a separate penalty of ₹10,000 to ₹1,00,000 can apply, on top of the late fee.
  • Late issue of Form 16: a penalty of ₹100 per day of delay past 15 June, for as long as the default continues.

Found a gap? Deposit the shortfall with interest as soon as it's identified — the interest cost only grows with time, and it's counted in whole months, so a short delay can cost more than expected. File a correction statement if the quarterly filing itself had an error.

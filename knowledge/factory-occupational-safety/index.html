Factory & occupational safety: the Factories Act, 1948 explained

When a workplace legally counts as a "factory," how Kerala licenses it through the Factories & Boilers Department, and why this is a separate track from the Shops Act — not an extra layer on top of it.

🏛️ Central law, Kerala-administered 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The Factories Act, 1948 regulates health, safety, welfare and working conditions in premises where a manufacturing process is carried on at a certain scale — licensed and inspected in Kerala by the Department of Factories & Boilers, not the Labour Commissionerate.
Why does it matter?
It is a distinct legal track from the Shops and Commercial Establishments Act. If your premises is a "factory," you register and comply under this Act instead of the Shops Act — getting this classification wrong is one of the more common compliance mix-ups we see.
Who does it apply to?
Premises running a manufacturing process with 10 or more workers using power, or 20 or more workers without power, on any day in the preceding 12 months.
What should an employer do first?
Confirm whether your operation meets the "factory" definition, and if it does, apply for a Factory Permit (10+ workers) or registration (fewer than 10, under the Section 85 notification) with the Kerala Department of Factories & Boilers before commencing or expanding operations.
Factories Act vs. Shops Act: not both, one or the other

These two Acts don't stack. The Shops and Commercial Establishments Act specifically excludes factories from its definition of "commercial establishment" — so once your premises meets the Factories Act's worker/power threshold, you register and comply under the Factories Act, and you do not also need Shops Act registration for that unit.

Where this gets confusing is a business with mixed operations — a small production unit attached to a retail showroom, for example. The manufacturing part is assessed against the Factories Act threshold on its own; the retail/office part may separately need Shops Act registration if it's a genuinely distinct establishment. This kind of mixed setup is worth checking with us individually rather than guessing.

Applicability

A premises is a "factory" under the Act if, on any day in the preceding 12 months, a manufacturing process is carried on:

  • With the aid of power and 10 or more workers are (or were) working, or
  • Without the aid of power and 20 or more workers are (or were) working.

"Manufacturing process" is defined broadly — it covers making, altering, repairing, finishing, packing, or even breaking up goods, not just heavy industrial production. A packaging unit, a printing press, or a food-processing operation can all qualify well before it looks like a traditional "factory" in the everyday sense.

Once a premises crosses this threshold, it stays covered even if worker count later dips — the same "once covered, stays covered" logic that applies under PF and ESI.

Branches: each factory premises is licensed on its own. If you run production at more than one Kerala location, each site needs its own permit/licence assessed against the same threshold independently.

Worker health, safety and welfare provisions

The Act's core purpose is protecting the people working inside a factory. Employers must provide and maintain, among other things:

AreaWhat's required
HealthCleanliness, ventilation and reasonable temperature, adequate lighting, disposal of waste and effluent, dust and fume control, drinking water at suitable points, latrines and urinals, spittoons.
SafetyFencing of dangerous machinery, safe means of access, precautions against fire, proper maintenance of hoists, lifts and pressure equipment, and safety officers/committees once headcount thresholds are crossed.
WelfareWashing facilities, first-aid boxes (broadly one per 150 workers), canteen once the factory employs more than 250 workers, crèche facility once it employs more than 30 women workers, and a welfare officer once it employs 500 or more workers.
Working conditionsDefined working hours, weekly holiday, rest intervals, and overtime pay — see below.

These aren't optional "nice to haves" — each is a specific statutory obligation, and inspectors check for them directly during a factory inspection.

Employer responsibilities

Core obligations: obtain the correct permit/licence before starting or expanding operations, maintain all the health/safety/welfare provisions above, follow the Act's working-hours rules, maintain prescribed registers (see our records & registers guide), display the required notices, report accidents and dangerous occurrences, and renew the licence every year.

  • A current factory licence is routinely checked in due-diligence, empanelment, and export/vendor certification processes — the same way a Shops Act certificate is for non-factory businesses.
  • Proper safety compliance reduces your exposure to accident-related liability, which under the Act carries some of the steepest fines of any labour law — see penalties below.
  • Kerala's Factories & Boilers Department also separately regulates boilers on the same premises where applicable, with its own certificate and annual inspection cycle.

Registration and licensing

When it becomes necessary: before commencing manufacturing operations that meet the factory threshold — this is a "before you start," not "after you're inspected," obligation.

Authority and portal: the Kerala Department of Factories & Boilers, through its online system at fabkerala.gov.in (applications processed via the department's IDMS portal).

Two-step process for 10+ worker units:

  • Factory Permit (before construction/installation): apply with a process flowchart, site plan, building plan showing lighting/ventilation/drainage, and machinery layout. This is approval-in-principle before you build or install.
  • Factory Licence (before starting operations): once construction is complete, apply in Form No. 2 along with a stability certificate (Form No. 3) and the prescribed fee.

Units under 10 workers: covered by a separate Section 85 notification — these still need to register and obtain a licence with the department, but skip the full permit stage that larger units go through.

Hazardous or polluting industries: also need a certificate of approval from the Chief Town Planner and a consent letter from the Kerala State Pollution Control Board before the department will process the application.

Government fees: payable through e-Treasury under the prescribed account head, scaled by factors including horsepower of machinery used and number of workers employed — get the current fee schedule confirmed for your specific unit before applying, since it's structured, not flat.

Working hours in a factory

The Factories Act sets its own working-hours rules for adult workers — distinct from, and generally stricter than, the Shops Act rules that apply to offices and retail:

RuleLimit
Weekly hours48 hours
Daily hours9 hours
Rest intervalAt least half an hour after every 5 hours of continuous work
Spread-overTotal spread of the working day, including rest intervals, generally capped at 10.5 hours
Weekly holidayOne full day off per week
Overtime payTwice the ordinary rate of wages for hours worked beyond the daily/weekly limit

These limits apply on top of the health/safety/welfare provisions — a factory that runs safe machinery but ignores the hours and overtime rules is still non-compliant.

Renewal

A Kerala factory licence runs on a calendar year and expires on 31 December every year, regardless of when in the year it was originally issued. Renewal applications should reach the Directorate at least 30 days before expiry — in practice, by 30 November.

Late renewal: applying by 31 December of the expiry year attracts an additional 25% over the normal fee; applying after 31 December attracts an additional 50%. The longer you leave it, the more it costs.

Changes to intimate: change in machinery/horsepower, change in nature of manufacturing process, change in worker count that affects your licence category, and change of occupier/management — these are filed as amendments, not a fresh application, in most cases.

Where this Act is headed

The central government's Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 — one of four new labour codes — took effect on 21 November 2025 and will eventually replace the Factories Act, 1948 nationally. Its central rules were notified in May 2026.

Kerala's own state-specific rules under this Code are still in draft and have not been notified. Until Kerala notifies them, factories here continue to be licensed and inspected under the Factories Act, 1948 and the Kerala Factories Rules, 1957, through the Department of Factories & Boilers exactly as described in this article. We'll update this guide as soon as Kerala's transition date is confirmed.

Non-compliance and correction

Penalties under the Factories Act are among the more serious in Indian labour law, because they're tied to worker safety, not just paperwork:

  • General contravention (Section 92): imprisonment up to 2 years, or a fine up to ₹1,00,000, or both — for the occupier and the manager individually. A continuing contravention adds a further fine of up to ₹1,000 for each day it continues.
  • Contravention causing death or serious injury: the fine cannot be less than ₹25,000 for a death, or ₹5,000 for a serious injury — these are minimums, not caps.
  • Repeat conviction (Section 94): imprisonment up to 3 years, or a fine of not less than ₹10,000 up to ₹2,00,000, or both.

If a licence has lapsed or was never obtained: apply or renew immediately, and get your safety/welfare provisions independently checked before an inspection does it for you — voluntary correction is treated far better than a default an inspector finds first, and it also limits your exposure if an accident happens in the meantime.

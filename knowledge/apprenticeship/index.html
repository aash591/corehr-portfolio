Apprenticeship compliance: the Apprentices Act, 1961 explained

Who must engage apprentices, how many, what to pay them, and why an apprentice is legally not the same thing as an employee — a common source of disputes we see employers walk into unintentionally.

🏛️ Central law — applies across India, including Kerala 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The Apprentices Act, 1961 requires certain employers to take on a set proportion of their workforce as apprentices each year — people undergoing structured, time-bound skill training, not regular employees.
Why does it matter?
Once your workforce hits 30, engagement is mandatory, not optional. Beyond the legal duty, apprentices are also the single biggest source of "are they staff or not?" confusion in payroll and HR — get the classification wrong and you risk PF/ESI/bonus disputes later.
Who does it apply to?
Any establishment with 30 or more people on its rolls (regular plus contract employees combined) must engage apprentices. Establishments with 4–29 may do so voluntarily. Establishments with 3 or fewer cannot engage apprentices under the Act at all.
What should an employer do first?
Work out your total workforce count, check whether you're above the 30 threshold, and if so, register on the Apprenticeship India portal and work out your quota band before you're asked for it in an inspection.
The distinction that causes the most disputes

An apprentice is a trainee, not a worker — this is written directly into Section 18 of the Act. While validly engaged under a registered apprenticeship contract, most labour laws that protect "workers" or "employees" simply don't apply to them in the same way.

In practice: an apprentice is not automatically entitled to PF, ESI, statutory bonus or gratuity through the apprenticeship itself, because they don't fall within the definition of "employee" under those Acts while they remain an apprentice. They're paid a stipend, governed by the Apprentices Act and their training contract — not the minimum wage law that applies to your regular workforce.

This only holds if the engagement is a genuine, registered apprenticeship. A person doing apprentice-like work without a registered contract, or kept on well past the agreed training period doing regular production/service work, can be reclassified as a worker by an inspector or a court — with PF, ESI, bonus and back-wage exposure attached. "Trainee" and "intern" job titles invented internally, outside this Act, get no such protection at all.

Applicability

The quota rules work on three bands, based on your total workforce — regular employees plus contract employees combined, not just permanent staff:

  • 30 or more: mandatory. You must engage apprentices, in a band of 2.5% to 15% of your workforce, every year.
  • 4 to 29: optional. You may engage apprentices if you want to, but there's no legal obligation.
  • 3 or fewer: not permitted. The Act doesn't allow engagement of apprentices below this size.

Within the 2.5%–15% band, at least 5% of your total workforce (including contract staff) must be reserved for fresher apprentices — people with no prior apprenticeship or work experience, including those holding only a skill certificate. This came from the Apprenticeship (Amendment) Rules, 2019.

Designated and optional trades: the government notifies certain "designated trades" with a fixed training curriculum. Since the 2019 amendment, employers can also run "optional trades" of their own design — industry-specific roles the government hasn't separately notified — for a training period of six months to three years, once approved.

Branches: the apprenticeship quota is normally worked out establishment by establishment. If you operate from more than one Kerala location, check with us on how your specific locations should be counted for quota purposes.

Apprentice stipend and rights

Apprentices are paid a stipend, not a wage under minimum wage law. The minimum stipend is fixed by the government and tiered by the apprentice's educational qualification at the time of engagement — it isn't a percentage of your local minimum wage.

Qualification tierApprox. current minimum stipend/month
School pass-outs, class 5–9Lowest tier of the schedule
Class 10 pass / class 12 passMid tier
ITI / vocational-certificate holders≈ ₹9,600
Diploma holders≈ ₹10,900
Graduate / degree apprentices≈ ₹12,300

The minimum stipend rises by 10% in the second year of training and a further 15% in the third year, on top of the qualification-tier base rate. These minimums are revised periodically by government notification — the figures above reflect the most recent published revision, so confirm the current schedule with us before finalising an offer.

Beyond the stipend, an apprentice undergoing training inside a factory is covered by the health, safety and welfare provisions of the Factories Act, 1948 as if they were a worker — cleanliness, ventilation, safety equipment and similar protections apply to them too. If an apprentice is injured in an accident arising out of their training, the employer is liable to pay compensation under the Employee's Compensation Act, just as for a regular employee. On successful completion, the apprentice receives a certificate of proficiency.

Employer responsibilities

Core obligations: work out and meet your quota band every year, register each apprentice under a written contract, pay the stipend on time, provide the structured training the scheme requires (not just general duties), and issue a completion certificate at the end.

  • Apprentices don't count toward headcount thresholds that trigger PF, ESI or Bonus Act coverage, since they aren't "employees" under those Acts during valid apprenticeship — but don't extend this logic to people who aren't genuinely apprentices under a registered contract.
  • Apprenticeship is one of the more cost-effective ways to build a skilled pipeline, since the stipend is well below a regular starting wage and part of it can be reimbursed under the National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme (see below).
  • Keeping your quota current avoids the compliance question coming up during a labour inspection, a tender empanelment, or a due-diligence review.

Registration

When it becomes necessary: once you cross the 30-employee threshold, or as soon as you decide to engage apprentices voluntarily below that.

Authority and portal: the Directorate General of Training (DGT), Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship, through the Apprenticeship India portal (apprenticeshipindia.gov.in), which also runs the National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme (NAPS).

  • Establishment: register your establishment on the portal once — business details, address, industry, workforce count.
  • Trade selection: pick from the notified designated trades relevant to your business, or apply for an optional trade if none fits.
  • Candidate sourcing: source apprentices directly, or through local ITIs, polytechnics and colleges via the portal's candidate database.
  • Contract: generate the apprenticeship contract on the portal for each apprentice; both employer and apprentice sign electronically, typically via Aadhaar-based OTP, before training starts. A minor apprentice's contract needs a parent or guardian's consent.

Government fees: registration on the portal itself carries no fee. Under NAPS, the government reimburses 25% of the prescribed stipend, up to ₹1,500 per apprentice per month, directly to the employer — this is a subsidy, not a compliance cost.

After registration: the contract must be registered with the Apprenticeship Adviser within the prescribed period of being signed. Keep training progress records for each apprentice, since these support the periodic returns due later.

Non-compliance and correction

The 2014 amendment to the Act removed imprisonment for most quota and procedural defaults, replacing it with fines under Section 30 — refusing to furnish information, failing to engage the required number of apprentices, or not maintaining proper training conditions are all treated as monetary offences today, not criminal ones. Obstructing an inspecting officer is treated more seriously and can still carry imprisonment in addition to a fine.

A further decriminalisation bill — the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Bill, 2026 — was introduced in Parliament in March 2026 and proposes moving first-time quota defaults to an advisory, with a formal penalty only from the second or later default. As of this writing, that bill has not yet been passed into law, so the existing fine-based position under Section 30 remains the current legal position.

If you're short of your quota: correcting it going forward is treated far better than waiting to be asked. Engage the shortfall for the current cycle and keep records showing the gap is being closed, rather than leaving it unaddressed until an inspection.

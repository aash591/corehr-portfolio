Contract labour & principal employer duties: the complete guide

What a Kerala business must do when it engages workers through a contractor — registration, the contractor's licence, and the liability that lands on you if the contractor defaults.

🏛️ CLRA Act, 1970 — still operative in Kerala 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
A framework regulating businesses that engage workers through a contractor rather than employing them directly — it requires the business (the "principal employer") to register, requires the contractor to hold a licence, and makes the principal employer answerable if the contractor fails on wages or welfare.
Why does it matter?
You can't outsource your legal exposure by outsourcing the workforce. If your contractor underpays or skips PF/ESI, the law can and does come back to you as the principal employer.
Who does it apply to?
In Kerala today, establishments engaging 20 or more contract workers on any day in the preceding 12 months, and contractors who engage 20 or more workers — this is the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 threshold, still the operative one in the state.
What should an employer do first?
Check your contract-workforce headcount against the 20-worker threshold, register as principal employer if you're covered, and verify every contractor's licence and PF/ESI status before, not after, engaging them.
Which law actually governs this in Kerala right now?

Nationally, the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 (CLRA) has been folded into the new Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code), which came into force on 21 November 2025, with its central rules notified on 8 May 2026. Under the OSH Code, the contract-labour threshold rises to 50 workers nationally and registration/licensing moves onto a unified Shram Suvidha Portal system.

But Kerala has not yet notified its own state rules under the OSH Code. As of mid-2026, Kerala was one of only two states/UTs (with West Bengal) that had not even begun the state-rules process, with the state government citing the need for more time to study the Codes. Until Kerala notifies its rules, the Kerala Labour Commissionerate continues to administer contract labour compliance under the CLRA Act, 1970 and the Kerala CLRA Rules, 1974 — including the 20-worker threshold and the existing Form I / contractor licence system. This article covers that currently-operative CLRA framework, and flags what changes once Kerala's OSH Code rules take effect.

Applicability

  • Principal employer: any establishment engaging 20 or more contract workers on any day in the preceding 12 months, through one or more contractors, must register under the CLRA Act.
  • Contractor: any contractor who engages 20 or more workers on any day in the preceding 12 months must hold a valid licence for that engagement.
  • Seasonal or intermittent work: if your contract headcount crosses 20 even briefly during the year, the threshold is met — it doesn't need to be a sustained headcount.
  • Once the OSH Code applies in Kerala, this threshold is expected to move to 50 workers, and the separate principal-employer registration is expected to fold into a single OSH Code establishment registration. Until Kerala's rules are notified, plan around the current 20-worker figure.

"Contract labour" here means workers engaged by or through a contractor, for work that is part of or incidental to your establishment's work, without being on your direct payroll. Genuinely independent service providers (a CA firm, an ad agency) are not contract labour in this sense — the test turns on control and integration into your operations, not just the invoice structure.

Worker benefits and rights

RightWhat it means
WagesNot less than the applicable minimum wage, paid on time and directly by the contractor (or by the principal employer if the contractor defaults).
Welfare amenitiesCanteen (where 100+ contract workers are engaged for 6 months or more), rest rooms, drinking water, latrines and washing facilities, and first aid, as prescribed for the establishment size.
Working conditionsThe same broad working-hours and safety protections applicable to the establishment's own workers, for the duration of the engagement.
Equal treatment on abolitionWhere contract labour is abolished for a particular type of work by government notification, absorption or other protections may apply depending on the specific notification.

These are baseline protections under the contract labour framework — they sit on top of, not instead of, the worker's separate entitlement to PF and ESI coverage if the wage and headcount thresholds under those Acts are met.

Principal employer responsibilities

Registering isn't the end of the obligation — it's the start of an ongoing duty to watch what your contractor is actually doing. The two provisions that matter most in practice are Sections 20 and 21 of the CLRA Act.

  • Section 20 — welfare amenities: if the contractor doesn't provide a required amenity (canteen, rest room, first aid, etc.) within the prescribed time, the principal employer must step in and provide it directly, then recover the cost from the contractor.
  • Section 21 — wages: if the contractor fails to pay wages on time, or pays below the applicable minimum wage, the principal employer must pay the shortfall directly to the workers, and can then recover that amount from the contractor. This liability applies even if the principal employer had no direct knowledge of the default — it can't be contracted away by a clause in your service agreement.
  • Nominate a representative to be present when the contractor disburses wages, so there's a documented check that payment actually happened correctly.
  • Maintain records of the contract labour engaged at your premises, even though the day-to-day registers are mainly the contractor's responsibility.
Why this catches employers off guard

Many businesses assume that once a worker is "the contractor's employee," wage and welfare compliance is entirely the contractor's problem. Sections 20 and 21 exist specifically because that assumption is wrong — the law treats the principal employer as the fallback guarantor precisely because contractors are more likely to default and harder to enforce against than the establishment actually benefiting from the work.

Contractor verification checklist

Before engaging any contractor, and periodically during the engagement, check the following. This is the single most effective way to avoid inheriting a contractor's default.

  • Licence validity: a current CLRA licence (Form IV, renewed via Form VII) covering the number of workers and the period of your engagement — not an expired or mismatched one.
  • PF code: an independent EPFO establishment code in the contractor's own name. If they don't have one, get a written undertaking that the workers will instead be enrolled under your PF code, and verify that actually happens.
  • ESI code: the same check for ESI, where applicable — an independent code, or coverage under your own code with documented enrolment.
  • Past compliance record: ask for recent PF/ESI challans or portal screenshots from their other engagements, and check for any pending labour department notices or disputes.
  • Minimum wage category: confirm the contractor is paying against the correct Kerala minimum wage category for the actual work being done, not a lower unrelated category.

Re-verify PF/ESI payment status periodically during the contract, not just once at onboarding — a contractor compliant on day one can lapse six months in, and the principal employer's exposure doesn't pause just because the initial check passed.

Registration and licensing

Principal employer registration: apply to the Registering Officer (Kerala Labour Commissionerate) in Form I, before engaging contract labour, once your headcount reaches the threshold. This registration doesn't expire on its own — it needs amendment if your establishment details change, and stays valid on an ongoing basis otherwise.

Contractor licence: the contractor applies separately, in Form IV, to the Licensing Officer, and must hold the licence before starting work at your premises. Unlike the principal employer's registration, a contractor's licence is typically valid for around 12 months and needs renewal (Form VII, filed before expiry) to stay current — this is one of the most common items to lapse silently mid-contract.

  • Principal employer: establishment registration proof, details of the work to be done through contract labour, and an estimate of the number of contract workers.
  • Contractor: the principal employer's certificate confirming the engagement, security deposit as prescribed, and the contractor's own establishment details.

Registers and records

Registers are mainly the contractor's responsibility, but the principal employer should know what they look like and ask to see them periodically, since the principal employer's own liability depends on what these registers show.

Non-compliance and penalties

Under the CLRA Act, contravening any provision, or breaching a condition of a contractor's licence, is punishable with imprisonment of up to 3 months, a fine of up to ₹1,000, or both — with a further ₹100-a-day fine for as long as a continuing contravention runs after conviction. Obstructing a labour inspector carries a separate penalty of up to 3 months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ₹500.

These figures look modest next to the underlying exposure: the wage-shortfall and welfare-amenity liability under Sections 20 and 21 isn't capped the same way, and a labour department finding of default can complicate future tenders, licences and audits well beyond the fine itself. Once Kerala's OSH Code rules take effect, penalties are expected to rise sharply — the Code's general penalty provision for unspecified contraventions runs from ₹2 lakh to ₹3 lakh, a large step up from the CLRA figures.

If you discover a gap — an unregistered engagement, a lapsed contractor licence, or a missed wage payment — regularise it and settle any shortfall directly with the affected workers as soon as it's found. Acting before a labour inspection or a worker complaint surfaces the issue is treated far more favourably than a default caught from outside.

POSH: the complete employer guide

What the Prevention of Sexual Harassment law requires from a Kerala employer — the Internal Committee, the Local Committee, complaint timelines, annual reporting, and what happens if you don't comply.

🏛️ Central law, applies nationwide 📅 Last verified: 19 September 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read

Quick answer

What is it?
The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (the POSH Act) requires employers to prevent, prohibit and redress sexual harassment of women at the workplace, mainly through a mandatory internal complaints mechanism.
Why does it matter?
It's not optional paperwork — failing to set up the required committee or act on its findings carries a specific fine under the Act, and the absence of a functioning committee is one of the first things checked in a labour dispute, tender due-diligence, or CSR/vendor audit.
Who does it apply to?
Every employer with 10 or more employees at any one office or branch — companies, partnerships, proprietorships, NGOs and government bodies alike. It protects women employees, trainees, interns, contract and daily-wage workers, and even visiting clients or customers.
What should an employer do first?
Constitute an Internal Committee with the correct composition, display its details and the law's consequences at a visible place, and put an annual awareness session on the calendar.
Internal Committee vs Local Committee — don't mix them up

Internal Committee (IC): set up by the employer, for workplaces with 10 or more employees. This is where most complaints go.

Local Committee (LC): set up by the District Officer, one per district. It handles complaints from workplaces with fewer than 10 employees, from domestic workers, and any complaint made against the employer himself — because in that case an internal mechanism can't be seen as independent.

Applicability

The POSH Act applies to every employer with 10 or more employees at a workplace, in any sector — private companies, government offices, NGOs, hospitals, educational institutions, and unincorporated businesses. There's no exemption for small or informal employers once headcount at a single office or branch reaches 10.

  • Who is protected: the Act protects only women — employees (regular, temporary, or on probation), trainees, interns, and women engaged on a contract, daily-wage, or voluntary basis, as well as women visiting the workplace as clients or customers.
  • What counts as "workplace": not just your office — it extends to any place visited during employment (client sites, field visits) and transport provided by the employer for the commute.
  • Multiple branches: each office or branch with 10 or more employees needs its own Internal Committee, or the employer can designate a Committee to cover multiple nearby units in some structures — check the specific setup with us before assuming one Committee covers everyone.
  • Below 10 employees: no Internal Committee is required, but a woman at that workplace can still take a complaint to the district's Local Committee.

Employee rights and protections

A woman who experiences sexual harassment at the workplace has the right to file a written complaint within 3 months of the incident (extendable by another 3 months if the Committee is satisfied there was good reason for the delay). She can request conciliation before a formal inquiry, though conciliation cannot involve any monetary settlement.

During the inquiry, she can ask the Committee for interim relief — such as a transfer for either party, or leave of up to 3 months for herself, without it affecting her service record. Her identity, and the identity of witnesses, are protected by confidentiality provisions under the Act; disclosing them without authorisation is itself a violation.

The Internal Committee and Local Committee

Internal Committee composition (minimum 4 members, at least half of them women):

  • Presiding Officer: a senior woman employee. If no senior woman is available at that office, the employer nominates one from another office or administrative unit.
  • At least 2 employee members: committed to the cause of women, or with legal knowledge, or experience in social work.
  • 1 external member: from an NGO or association working for women's rights, or otherwise familiar with issues of sexual harassment — this person cannot be an employee of your organisation.

Committee members hold office for a term not exceeding 3 years from nomination. The external member is typically paid a sitting fee or allowance for attending proceedings, as fixed by the employer.

Local Committee is constituted by the District Officer for the district — in Kerala, this function generally sits with the District Social Justice Officer under the Social Justice Department. It has a Chairperson (a woman eminent in the field of social work and committed to women's rights), a member from an NGO working on women's issues, two other members with relevant legal or social work background (at least one a woman), and an ex-officio member from the district's social welfare or women-and-child-development machinery. The Kerala High Court has directed authorities to reconstitute the Local Complaints Committee promptly whenever a term lapses, so its composition stays valid on an ongoing basis.

Complaint and inquiry process

InquiryMust be completed within 90 days of the complaint
Inquiry reportSubmitted to the employer/District Officer within 10 days of completing the inquiry
Action on the reportEmployer/District Officer must act on the recommendations within 60 days of receiving it
AppealEither party can appeal to the appropriate court or tribunal within 90 days of the recommendations

Throughout the process, both the complainant and respondent are entitled to be heard, and either side can bring one support person (not a lawyer, in most cases) to assist them. If the Committee finds the complaint to be false or malicious with clear bad intent — not merely unproven — it can recommend action against the complainant, though this is applied cautiously and is not the default outcome of an unproven complaint.

Employer responsibilities

Section 19 of the Act sets out specific, ongoing duties for every employer — these are separate from simply having a Committee on paper.

  • Provide a safe working environment and take steps to prevent harassment before it happens.
  • Display, at a conspicuous place, the Committee's composition, contact details, and the penal consequences of sexual harassment.
  • Organise workshops and awareness programmes at regular intervals for all employees, and orientation/skill-building sessions for Committee members — annually, at minimum, is the accepted standard even though the Act itself just says "regular intervals".
  • Provide the Committee with the facilities it needs to complete an inquiry — meeting space, access to records, and cooperation from other staff as witnesses.
  • Assist the woman if she chooses to also file a police complaint under the Indian Penal Code / Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.
  • Treat sexual harassment as misconduct under service rules and initiate action accordingly once the Committee's findings support it.
  • Monitor that the Committee submits its report within the statutory timeline, and act on it within 60 days.

Annual report and recurring compliance

The Internal Committee (or Local Committee) must prepare an annual report at the end of every calendar year and submit it to the employer and to the District Officer, under Section 21. This applies even in a year with zero complaints — a nil report is still required.

The report covers: the number of complaints received during the year, the number disposed of, the number pending for more than 90 days (with reasons), and the number of workshops or awareness programmes conducted. Many companies also disclose their POSH complaint numbers in the statutory Board's Report filed under the Companies Act, since this is a standard disclosure item for companies with a board.

Non-compliance and penalties

Under Section 26, failing to constitute an Internal Committee, failing to act on its recommendations, or otherwise contravening the Act or its rules attracts a fine of up to ₹50,000 for a first offence.

A second or subsequent conviction can double the fine, and — separately — can put at risk any registration, licence, or approval that the business needs to operate, since the Act allows for its cancellation, withdrawal or non-renewal on repeat default.

If you discover a gap — an unconstituted Committee, a lapsed external member's term, or missed annual reports — the practical fix is to correct it immediately rather than wait for an inspection or a complaint to surface the gap first. A functioning, correctly composed Committee at the time a complaint is filed matters more than a perfect compliance history before that point.
